Hi, I'm Silas Brown, I'm a computer scientist and this is why I think AI cannot own copyright on the works that it generates.

I'd like to start my story by talking about a real, not artificial, intelligence called William Lovelock. Now he was a doctor of music and in the 1940s he wrote some textbooks that taught classical music harmony, page after page of rules like this about what you can do and what you can't do for it to work in classical music harmony.

Now 50 years later I was in school, I was at a school that had these books and they were using them for A-level. I decided I would write this into computer code. So I coded and I coded and I coded, and people thought I was going mad perhaps, and thousands of lines later I had this.

Now that might seem impressive for a few minutes, but if you let it carry on, it will carry on writing more and more and more of the same sort of thing, which gets a bit bland after a while. Now this might have helped me get into Cambridge to do computer science but it's not exactly the next Beethoven.

Now this isn't a criticism of William Lovelock because William Lovelock never intended his rules to be applied mechanically by a computer. He was writing to help music students and he'd done a lot of research into classical harmony and what classical composers tend to do and what they tend not to do, and he thought this information would be very useful for his students to help them, but that's not to say that he expected his students to follow the rules exactly mechanically without putting in their own creative streak. How do we know that? Well listen to what happens when William Lovelock actually writes his own composition.

[William Lovelock: Concerto for Trumpet and Orchestra movement 2, G minor section (excerpt)]

And that's all you get because this is copyright and I don't want to infringe the copyright of the Lovelock estate and the performers by giving you the whole thing. I just gave you a short extract to make a point; that's called fair use. Now hopefully everyone here will agree with me that the music created by William Lovelock himself is more creative than the music generated by his rules.

Now why is that? Why can't my program write music that's as good as the guy who wrote the rule book? Could it be that we need more rules? Maybe to give poor William Lovelock a break we could come out with some way of automatically discovering the rules so we can feed thousands of hours of classical music into the computer and we have some way of getting the computer to automatically extract the most likely rules.

Now I've used automatically generated rules before, let me show you what automatically generated rules can be like. This is my Google Chrome extension for adding Chinese pronunciation and dictionary entries to websites. Let's put in a phrase that uses a Chinese character [差] that can be pronounced in three different ways depending on context, chāi, chā or chà.  And in this case my program has got it right.  But let's see what happens if I delete that comma. When I delete the comma it goes completely wrong. Why does it go wrong like that? Because the underlying rule that my program automatically generated was that this character means 'send' if it occurs within three characters of the word 'to go'. Why three characters? Because all the input text that I gave it fit that pattern. It can't extrapolate outside the pattern in the training set. If I put the comma back in, then it works perfectly fine again. That's what automatically generated rules can be like. Very impressive sometimes, can go wrong other times. Of course now I've seen that particular fault, I can actually go in there and tweak that rule for it so that future versions won't make that particular mistake again. But this does give us some idea of what automatically generated rules might be like. You can generate thousands and thousands of them but at the end of the day there's still going to be glitches.

And yet people seem to think that all we need is a little more work and will have proper self-awareness. It's interesting if you look at computer villains in films. The script writers of films would like to be able to say that there is a computer, that was designed to be a nice computer and not hurt anybody, but unknown to its designers, it developed self-awareness and it decided to do something bad. This has been done in quite a few films and I've just picked out two; they are very popular films. One is a classic and one is this year's film, and in both cases they borrowed words from real AI research and implied that if you just take that a little bit further you will have your self-aware character. So the 1968 film had the heuristic algorithmic computer or HAL. Why heuristic algorithmic? Well quite possibly because in the 1960s there were research projects like Heuristic Dendral to make decisions about chemistry that used heuristic algorithms, which everyone was getting excited about because it seemed we wouldn't have to program everything. We just write these heuristics and the AI can take care of the rest. It was forgivable to think that all you have to do is a little bit more work and you might have something like HAL. But now we know that heuristic algorithms are much more limited than that, this year's film couldn't get away with saying heuristic algorithms, so they used the word generative, that's more up-to-date. In fact they called it the model three generative android. Why model three generative? Well maybe that was something to do with the fact that when they were making the film the generative pre-trained transformer GPT-3 was showcased by a company called OpenAI. As if to say all we need is a slightly better version of ChatGPT and out pops M3GAN. "That's insane, right?"  Well "I'm afraid we can't do that" because it turns out today's generative systems have limits just as the 1960s heuristic systems had limits.

Now it reminds me of the joke about a teacher who put a plate or a tile near a heat source and of course you expect the part that's nearest the heat source to become warmer, but he had a student feel it and the student found that the part furthest away from the heat source was warmer and he asked the student why that is and the student says maybe it's heat conduction, but the actual answer is because the teacher turned the plates around. Now Eliezer Yudkowsky called this "guessing the teacher's password". The student was tricked into thinking that the answer must be something we've studied in science, and we've studied about conduction, convection and radiation, so the answer is probably either conduction or convection or radiation. This is a solid so I'm going to guess conduction. But actually it was a trick question.

Today's generative AI systems are very much like that student being tricked into coming up with a word that they've learned, when they don't actually understand what is happening. It reminds me of what's being called cargo cult mentality. In the Second World War some armies needed to use islands in the Pacific, and they landed aeroplanes and boats and things on these islands, and some of the people who were native to those islands didn't understand how technology works, but they saw people build runways and control-towers and things and then aeroplanes landed, and they said oh, that's a good idea, I wonder if I can do that too, so I'll build a runway and I'll build a control tower, but then an aeroplane doesn't land for me and I don't understand why that is. I made my runway as good as I could. I made my control tower as good as I could. I even put somebody up there wearing bamboo headphones. Everything was imitated exactly, and yet I don't get aeroplanes. Now in order to understand why imitating the runway doesn't get you aeroplanes, you have to understand how the system of aeroplanes really works, and that was the insight they were missing, and it's similar with today's AI. It may be able to come up with an impressive-looking imitation but it has no real understanding.

Now what really are we missing here? Let's talk about Raymond Cattell. Raymond Cattell came up with the idea of fluid intelligence as being different from crystallised intelligence. The idea is that in a person we have fluid intelligence which, is intelligence that will help us work out new things, and we also have crystallised intelligence, things like general knowledge and reasoning ability based on information that we've learned based on a prior application of fluid intelligence. And when we make decisions, we use both our fluid intelligence and also our crystallised intelligence. We use both. So applying this to creativity, a human artist can look at the creations of several other humans and be influenced by them and also be influenced by their own life, and they can apply their fluid intelligence to all of that, and their crystallised intelligence, and create something new. And that's how we get jokes like copying one source is plagiarism but copying lots of sources is scholarship. Of course it's more complicated than that because we're applying our fluid intelligence to all those sources, we're not just copying them verbatim. It gets worse when you're a lexicographer. I maintain a Chinese-English dictionary and I have thousands of sources for it but I still apply my fluid intelligence to it.

But when we bring AI into the picture, using any of the techniques so far developed, what we have is this. The fluid intelligence of the person looking at the sources has been replaced by an automatic training process, and that automatic training process produces pure crystallised intelligence with no fluid intelligence attached to it at all. What that crystallised intelligence generates: is it really a new creation? I'm not convinced. Of course if the AI goes wrong and copies too much from one particular source, then it could be infringing copyright, or more technically the person who uses it could be infringing copyright if they publish the result. But we're not really creating new copyrightable material here unless the person who's using it puts in substantial creative input of their own into the process, because true creativity requires the application of fluid intelligence, and if you have pure crystallised intelligence from an AI system all that can do is basically mash up the existing data in a non-creative way and it may appear impressive to begin with but what you're seeing is the creativity in the original training set that was given to this thing.

But the law can have surprising details and there is a little-known section 178 of the 1988 Copyright Designs and Patents Act of the UK that might imply automatically generated things are copyrightable. This section was meant to protect things like generated weather maps and the copyright is not held by the AI of course, it's held by the creators of the program. But this has not been used very much and other countries don't have it; it's not in the Berne copyright convention. The Berne copyright convention's opinion is basically that "sweat of the brow" works, works that have been done without any creative input, are not copyrightable.

In fact you can't even copyright ideas, you can only copyright creative presentations. So William Lovelock can copyright his harmony books but he can't copyright the actual idea of classical harmony. All the research he did, he can't actually copyright that; he can copyright the particular way he presented it in his particular books, but he can't copyright the idea itself. I come along and I rewrite it all in C++ computer code. Again I can copyright that code, but I can't copyright the underlying idea, and therefore I can't copyright the things it generates unless of course I use it as part of a larger work in which I have non-trivial creative input of my own.

Now there are other laws to protect other things, for example there are patents to protect inventions, and there are database rights laws, but as far as copyright is concerned, I believe that AI systems might be able to infringe copyright if they go wrong, but they can't create newly copyrighted material all by themselves, because they are pure crystallised intelligence without having the aid of fluid intelligence. Thank you.
